LESSON 1-1: Being right is not enough
A trade needs room to survive before the expected reversal arrives.
SOURCE: Opening: the old trader and the yen | Printed pp. 1–10 | PDF pp. 27–36
KEY IDEAS:
- A plausible forecast can still produce a dangerous position.
- Financing and adverse moves matter before the final outcome.
- A recovery does not prove the risk was sensible.
BOOK CONTEXT: The opening currency narrative follows a trader through losses, strained borrowing, a reversal and renewed exposure. It makes the difference between an eventual recovery and a safe process unusually vivid.
The pressure inside a position
The narrator watches currency quotes while brokers, banks and clients constrain what he can do. His view is only one part of the trade: the size, funding and time available to withstand losses also matter.
The lesson to carry forward
Do not assess a method solely by whether one story ends with a recovery. Ask how close it came to a loss that would have made further trading impossible. This is our analytical takeaway from the episode, not a position-sizing formula prescribed by the author.
OUR EXAMPLE: A $10,000 account controls $100,000 of exposure. A 5% adverse move loses about $5,000 before costs. A later recovery is irrelevant if a financing constraint forces the position closed first.
OUR EXERCISE: Write down the move that would force you to exit before your forecast had time to work. Include financing and liquidity constraints.
SELF-CHECK: What does a profitable ending establish?
A. The position was safely sized.
B. The realized outcome was profitable; the process still needs examination.
C. The forecast must always work.
ANSWER: B. An outcome is one observation. It does not erase the exposure or financing risks along the way.

LESSON 1-2: A panic is also a funding crisis
Price risk and the ability to finance a position can deteriorate together.
SOURCE: Chapter 2 · Panic and bad luck | Printed pp. 47–49 | PDF pp. 73–75
KEY IDEAS:
- A quoted price does not guarantee a willing buyer.
- Borrowing can become scarce when you need it most.
- Examine funding and liquidity alongside direction.
BOOK CONTEXT: The chapter opens with the 1907 panic, describing scarce lending, distressed crowds and exchange closures. Its account shows how a market decline becomes more destructive when investors cannot raise cash or sell positions.
Think in two dimensions
The teaching takeaway is to ask two questions separately: what happens to the asset, and what happens to your ability to hold it? An optimistic price forecast does not answer the second question. Historical episodes can suggest stress scenarios; they do not establish the probability of the next crisis.
OUR EXAMPLE: A hypothetical asset falls 8%. At the same time, the lender demands more collateral. The cash request may force a sale even if the investor believes the asset will recover.
OUR EXERCISE: For a hypothetical position, list three reasons you could be forced out: a price move, a financing change and a loss of liquidity.
SELF-CHECK: Which additional question belongs beside a price forecast?
A. Will the chart look attractive?
B. Can the position still be financed and traded under stress?
C. Can every decline be explained afterward?
ANSWER: B. Funding and liquidity can constrain a trade before the forecast is resolved.

LESSON 1-3: Do not confuse endurance with judgment
Fatigue and attachment are part of the opening story.
SOURCE: Opening · The old trader and the yen | Printed pp. 7–10 | PDF pp. 33–36
KEY IDEAS:
- Long hours do not establish an informational advantage.
- Personal attachment can change the meaning of a trade.
- Review decisions before celebrating an outcome.
BOOK CONTEXT: In the opening, the narrator describes fear, family pressure and prolonged sleepless attention to currency quotes. After an improvement, he takes further exposure and regrets it. The account reveals strain rather than a frictionless trading system.
Separate effort from evidence
A decision can feel justified because it has consumed time, money or pride. Those are sunk commitments, not new evidence. Our exercise is to describe the trade as if it belonged to someone else, then identify what information actually supports it.
OUR EXAMPLE: A researcher spends three weeks on a hypothesis and feels obliged to keep it after contradictory results. The three weeks are effort already spent; they do not make the hypothesis more accurate.
OUR EXERCISE: Write a neutral one-paragraph account of a decision you felt emotionally committed to. Remove words such as “deserve” and “must.”
SELF-CHECK: What should change a hypothesis?
A. The amount of effort already spent.
B. New evidence relevant to the claim.
C. The desire to recover a previous loss.
ANSWER: B. Evidence bears on the claim. Prior effort and disappointment do not.

LESSON 1-4: Being contrarian does not remove danger
The 1987 account contains a warning inside the buying story.
SOURCE: Chapter 4 · Actual losses | Printed pp. 123–124 | PDF pp. 149–150
KEY IDEAS:
- Fear in the crowd is not sufficient evidence.
- Adding exposure can worsen an already dangerous situation.
- Read the adverse path as carefully as the recovery.
BOOK CONTEXT: The author describes buying stock-index futures during the 1987 crash, losing rapidly and adding more after hearing clients’ concerns. The next day brings both a higher margin requirement and a recovery that permits an exit.
Examine the sequence
This episode makes the appeal and danger of contrarian action visible together. The later rebound is part of the story, but so are the immediate loss and the funding pressure. It cannot establish that every panic is an asymmetric buying opportunity.
OUR EXAMPLE: In a fictional stress event, an asset rebounds after a 30% fall. A participant who was forced to close after the first 20% decline receives no benefit from that rebound.
OUR EXERCISE: Write a timeline of the episode: entry, additional exposure, adverse move, margin change and exit. Identify where survival was uncertain.
SELF-CHECK: Which fact would be unsafe to omit from the account?
A. The intervening losses and margin increase.
B. The color of the trading-room furniture.
C. The eventual rebound alone.
ANSWER: A. The path and constraints determine whether the recovery was available to the participant.

LESSON 1-5: Different positions can share one risk
Several instruments can be exposed to the same event.
SOURCE: Chapter 4 · Actual losses | Printed pp. 124–125 | PDF pp. 150–151
KEY IDEAS:
- Count economic exposures, not just positions.
- A single announcement can affect several markets.
- A plausible political forecast can fail abruptly.
BOOK CONTEXT: The chapter describes simultaneous bond, stock-index and oil positions before diplomatic talks. When the talks fail, several prices move against the portfolio together. The author draws attention to the unpredictability of political developments.
Find the common assumption
Different instruments do not automatically mean different sources of risk. Write the assumption behind each position, then check whether the same event challenges several of them. The aim of this exercise is to understand concentration in a hypothetical portfolio.
OUR EXAMPLE: Three fictional positions all benefit if an agreement is reached. Holding three tickers has not diversified the risk of failed negotiations.
OUR EXERCISE: For three hypothetical positions, write the event that helps each and the event that hurts each. Circle repeated events.
SELF-CHECK: What is evidence of a shared risk?
A. Different instrument names.
B. Several positions relying on the same outcome.
C. Different entry prices.
ANSWER: B. A common dependency can connect positions across markets.

LESSON 1-6: An exit instruction is part of the trade
Read the author’s stop-order argument in its historical context.
SOURCE: Chapter 4 · Actual losses and order instructions | Printed pp. 125–126 | PDF pp. 151–152
KEY IDEAS:
- Execution instructions must be unambiguous.
- An exit rule can have costs and limitations.
- The author’s preference is not a universal safety rule.
BOOK CONTEXT: The author recounts a bond stop order followed by a rebound and criticizes stop-loss use, with an exception related to margin capacity. In the same passage, he emphasizes clear order communication and avoiding carelessness under pressure.
Separate the claim from its proof
The episode explains his concern about how orders are exposed and executed. A single adverse stop-and-rebound story does not settle which exit rule is best across markets. Our takeaway is to identify the execution assumptions and compare alternatives under the same conditions.
OUR EXAMPLE: A hypothetical instruction “sell near 100” leaves uncertainty about price, timing and order type. Specifying the intended conditions makes the instruction clear, but does not guarantee that the desired price is available.
OUR EXERCISE: Write an imaginary exit instruction. List what it specifies and what market conditions could prevent the intended outcome.
SELF-CHECK: What is directly supported by the order-communication lesson?
A. Never control losses.
B. Make instructions clear and examine execution assumptions.
C. An exit always fills at the displayed price.
ANSWER: B. Clarity addresses avoidable ambiguity without pretending that execution risk disappears.

LESSON 2-1: The oracle problem
Prestige and ambiguity can make a forecast seem stronger than it is.
SOURCE: Chapter 3 · The Delphi oracle and science | Printed pp. 81–83 | PDF pp. 107–109
KEY IDEAS:
- An impressive setting is not a test of accuracy.
- Ambiguous forecasts leave room for reinterpretation.
- Ask what a claim means before asking whether it worked.
BOOK CONTEXT: The chapter uses Delphi’s authority, atmosphere and cultural status to introduce the problem of prediction. The analogy invites scrutiny of the institutions and presentation surrounding a forecast.
Make the language examinable
“The market will be volatile, but opportunities will emerge” fits many outcomes. For our study purposes, a useful forecast needs a defined event, a horizon and a rule for judging success. This operational definition is a teaching extension of the chapter’s skepticism.
OUR EXAMPLE: Compare “a reversal is near” with “the closing price will be above today’s close after five trading sessions.” The second can be evaluated consistently, even if it turns out to be wrong.
OUR EXERCISE: Rewrite a vague prediction into a specific event and deadline. State exactly what would count as failure.
SELF-CHECK: Which prediction is easier to evaluate?
A. A major change may be coming.
B. Expect a difficult but promising period.
C. The price will close higher after five sessions.
ANSWER: C. A defined outcome and horizon limit reinterpretation.

LESSON 2-2: A hypothesis must be able to fail
Scientific reasoning includes correction, not just confirmation.
SOURCE: Chapter 3 · Method and applied science | Printed pp. 99–101 | PDF pp. 125–127
KEY IDEAS:
- Observe and measure before generalizing.
- State a possible disconfirming result.
- Revise a theory when its test fails.
BOOK CONTEXT: The “Method” passage describes observation, measurement, hypotheses, testing and revision. It invokes falsifiability and warns that an explanation compatible with every possible state is not a scientific theory.
Write the rejection rule first
A claim is difficult to learn from if every unfavorable result can be explained away. Before testing it, define the measurement and the result that would make you reject or narrow it. This does not eliminate uncertainty; it makes disagreement with the data visible.
OUR EXAMPLE: Suppose the claim is that a defined event raises the next-day average return. Decide the event threshold, comparison group and evaluation period before seeing the outcomes.
OUR EXERCISE: Choose one belief and complete: “I would revise this belief if I observed…”
SELF-CHECK: Which statement is hardest to falsify?
A. The effect is positive in this specified sample.
B. The effect will always work, except whenever conditions are unsuitable.
C. The effect disappears after costs.
ANSWER: B. An undefined exception can protect a claim from every possible result.

LESSON 2-3: Galton: define what you are counting
A clean question comes before a large spreadsheet.
SOURCE: Chapter 14 · Counting problems and how to count | Printed pp. 454–457 | PDF pp. 480–483
KEY IDEAS:
- Write the question before collecting observations.
- Use categories that stay consistent.
- A bigger dataset cannot fix an unclear definition.
BOOK CONTEXT: The counting discussion acknowledges perceptual and judgment errors. In the Galton passage, it emphasizes writing down the measurement question before recording and separating cases to avoid confusion.
Keep the measurement stable
An observation should mean the same thing from the first row to the last. If “panic,” “rebound” or “strong day” changes meaning midway, the summary statistic mixes different events. Record a definition and a consistent rule before collecting the data.
OUR EXAMPLE: A “large down day” could mean a fall of at least 2% from the previous close. If later rows use an intraday decline instead, the categories are no longer comparable.
OUR EXERCISE: Define one event using an observable threshold, a time interval and an explicit unit.
SELF-CHECK: What comes first?
A. A precise counting question.
B. A visually impressive chart.
C. A preferred conclusion.
ANSWER: A. The question and measurement rule determine what the observations represent.

LESSON 2-4: A wise saying still needs a test
An impressive trading legend can leave out decisive evidence.
SOURCE: Chapter 1 · Lessons from Livermore | Printed pp. 23–25 | PDF pp. 49–51
KEY IDEAS:
- A memorable maxim is a claim, not a measurement.
- Include adverse outcomes when judging a legend.
- Turn advice into a reproducible question.
BOOK CONTEXT: The Livermore discussion contrasts remembered brilliance with omitted failures. Niederhoffer then argues for evaluating, testing and analyzing seemingly sensible theories rather than accepting them on the strength of a story.
Translate the maxim
“Successful traders do X” has hidden questions: which traders, what counts as success, how X is identified and whether unsuccessful traders also do it. A fair test needs both the favored examples and a relevant comparison group.
OUR EXAMPLE: If ten famous winners kept journals, that is interesting. If almost every unsuccessful participant also kept one, the observation alone does not show that journaling explains success.
OUR EXERCISE: Choose a trading maxim. Write its hidden denominator and an observation that would weaken it.
SELF-CHECK: What is missing from a collection of successful examples?
A. A relevant comparison including failures.
B. A more impressive author name.
C. A stronger slogan.
ANSWER: A. Selection of winners can conceal whether the proposed feature actually distinguishes them.

LESSON 2-5: Explanation and prediction are different
A model that fits old charts may not help with unseen data.
SOURCE: Chapter 10 · Deceptive technical models | Printed pp. 296–298 | PDF pp. 322–324
KEY IDEAS:
- Explaining a past chart is easier than predicting a new one.
- Ask when the model’s choices were made.
- Freeze a rule before evaluating later outcomes.
BOOK CONTEXT: In a conversation with a technical analyst, the author presses the distinction between explaining stored charts and predicting. The discussion of selected trend lines and recurring models exposes the flexibility of retrospective interpretation.
Reserve something you have not seen
Our research extension is to separate data used to formulate a rule from data used to evaluate it. If every revision is chosen because it improves the same historical result, the result also measures the researcher’s ability to fit that history.
OUR EXAMPLE: A fictional rule is adjusted ten times on January–June data. Testing the final rule on untouched July–December data asks a different and more demanding question.
OUR EXERCISE: Design a study with a development sample and a later evaluation sample. Write what may change before evaluation and what must remain fixed.
SELF-CHECK: Why preserve an unseen sample?
A. To guarantee success.
B. To challenge whether the rule works beyond the data used to invent it.
C. To make the historical chart smoother.
ANSWER: B. An unseen evaluation reduces the advantage gained from repeated fitting to the same history.

LESSON 2-6: Count behavior in its context
The same player can behave differently under different incentives.
SOURCE: Chapter 14 · Counting education | Printed pp. 456–457 | PDF pp. 482–483
KEY IDEAS:
- Record context alongside the outcome.
- Averages can mix different kinds of contest.
- Incentives may change how people act.
BOOK CONTEXT: The father’s sporting records distinguish scores, shots and errors. The discussion also contrasts formal and informal matches and describes how a player’s behavior changes with the score.
Avoid an average that hides the question
If performance changes with context, one overall average may conceal the relevant comparison. Define the groups before viewing the result and keep group sizes visible. Context helps refine a question; it must not become an unlimited excuse for excluding inconvenient cases.
OUR EXAMPLE: A fictional player attacks more when behind than when ahead. Combining both states into one shot-success rate hides the change in choices.
OUR EXERCISE: For an everyday observation, record one context variable such as time, setting or task difficulty along with the outcome.
SELF-CHECK: What can contextual records reveal?
A. Why every outcome was inevitable.
B. Differences hidden inside an overall average.
C. A universal explanation without further tests.
ANSWER: B. Separating defined contexts can expose differences that an aggregate conceals.

LESSON 3-1: The rules are part of the opportunity
The environment can interrupt a plan that looks sound on paper.
SOURCE: Chapter 1 · Training at Brighton Beach | Printed pp. 11–13 | PDF pp. 37–39
KEY IDEAS:
- Check rules, interruptions and execution constraints.
- A pause can change the outcome of a contest.
- Translate an anecdote into a question, not a universal law.
BOOK CONTEXT: Early street-game stories include a dispute over a pause while the weather changes. The author connects this with a later exchange interruption during a storm and an adverse move before trading resumes.
Model the actual arena
A plan may assume immediate execution, continuous trading or an unchanged rulebook. Reality may not provide them. The lesson is to include operational constraints in the description of an opportunity, rather than treating them as an afterthought.
OUR EXAMPLE: A paper trade assumes an exit at the next quote. An interruption means the next available price arrives much later. The paper result and the executable result can diverge.
OUR EXERCISE: List the assumptions that make a hypothetical exit possible. Which one would fail during a closure?
SELF-CHECK: What does the street-game analogy help you notice?
A. Operational conditions can change the result.
B. Weather predicts every price move.
C. A correct forecast guarantees execution.
ANSWER: A. The relevant lesson concerns constraints, not a universal weather rule.

LESSON 3-2: Checkers and chess: learn the new frame
An old reference point may become a blind spot.
SOURCE: Chapter 7 · Lessons from board games | Printed pp. 200–202 | PDF pp. 226–228
KEY IDEAS:
- Skills transfer imperfectly between games.
- Check which assumptions belong to the old setting.
- An analogy should clarify differences as well as similarities.
BOOK CONTEXT: The author describes difficulty moving from checkers-style thinking to chess. He then compares this with carrying a familiar investment framework into a different market, warning that the arena’s mechanics matter.
Inspect the transfer
Instead of asking whether two activities look alike, ask which rules they share. Holding periods, financing, settlement and objectives can differ. A useful analogy suggests a hypothesis; it does not exempt the new setting from investigation.
OUR EXAMPLE: Owning a company share and holding a futures contract both involve price exposure, but their financing, maturity and settlement mechanics are not interchangeable.
OUR EXERCISE: Choose two markets or two competitive games. Name one transferable skill and two rules that differ.
SELF-CHECK: When moving to a new arena, what deserves attention?
A. Only the similarities.
B. The mechanics and assumptions that change.
C. The reputation earned in the old arena.
ANSWER: B. Past competence does not remove the need to learn a different rulebook.

LESSON 3-3: Independent judgment needs feedback
Resistance to convention is useful only when you keep learning.
SOURCE: Chapter 5 · Winning and self-reliance | Printed pp. 147–149 | PDF pp. 173–175
KEY IDEAS:
- Confidence can help resist inherited dogma.
- Tradition and results are separate questions.
- Keep independence connected to evidence.
BOOK CONTEXT: The opening of this chapter connects support from family, music and sport with resistance to dogma. Its account of squash traditions and changing coaching practices also shows that conventions can evolve.
Avoid making dissent a new dogma
Our takeaway is that being contrary is not itself a method. Independent judgment should let you question authority and also question yourself. A result that contradicts your preferred position deserves the same attention as one that contradicts the consensus.
OUR EXAMPLE: Two analysts disagree with a popular rule. One defines a test; the other treats popularity as proof that the rule is wrong. Only the first has created a way to learn.
OUR EXERCISE: Identify a conventional belief you doubt. Record both the evidence against it and the strongest evidence in its favor.
SELF-CHECK: What makes dissent informative?
A. Disagreeing with every majority view.
B. Testing the disputed claim against evidence.
C. Having a confident personality.
ANSWER: B. Independence helps formulate questions; evidence helps resolve them.

LESSON 3-4: Racket sports: practice the component
Repeated drills can expose weaknesses that matches conceal.
SOURCE: Chapter 1 · Racket training | Printed pp. 28–31 | PDF pp. 54–57
KEY IDEAS:
- Break a skill into repeatable components.
- Practice both the comfortable and difficult side.
- Keep a record that makes improvement visible.
BOOK CONTEXT: The author describes solo racket drills: repeated forehands and backhands, work against different walls, movement and training diaries. He contrasts this with spending all practice time playing matches.
Design deliberate practice
The useful transfer is a small task with clear feedback. In research, that might mean classifying an event consistently or checking calculations accurately. More hours matter only when they include the work that actually needs improvement.
OUR EXAMPLE: Instead of drawing twenty new charts, a learner classifies the same ten events twice using a written rule and investigates every disagreement.
OUR EXERCISE: Choose one skill for a ten-minute drill. Define the task, the feedback and the error you want to reduce.
SELF-CHECK: Which practice task has clearer feedback?
A. Repeat a defined classification and check disagreements.
B. Consume unrelated material for longer.
C. Avoid the difficult component.
ANSWER: A. A bounded component makes errors and changes easier to identify.

A decision changes the position other participants face.
SOURCE: Chapter 7 · Rules of the game | Printed pp. 211–214 | PDF pp. 237–240
KEY IDEAS:
- Consider the response your action permits.
- A local gain can create a new weakness.
- Simplicity of rules does not imply simplicity of strategy.
BOOK CONTEXT: The board-game maxims emphasize economical moves, attention to details, time and the changing position. They also warn that advancing without adequate defense exposes weaknesses.
Draw a small decision tree
A board game has specified legal moves; a market does not offer the same complete information. Still, considering responses can prevent one-sided reasoning. List plausible responses and your constraints, keeping unknown incentives labeled as unknown.

A. Only the hoped-for outcome.
B. Plausible responses and the position they create.
C. An assumption that nobody else adapts.
ANSWER: B. Strategic reasoning includes how the decision changes the choices available to others.

LESSON 3-6: Chess: know when the position has changed
Prepared plans and flexibility belong together.
SOURCE: Chapter 7 · Qualities of a winner | Printed pp. 224–227 | PDF pp. 250–253
KEY IDEAS:

Write an adaptation trigger
A plan is useful because it makes assumptions explicit. If those assumptions fail, updating is not the same as abandoning discipline. The disciplined step is to state which new observation changes the plan and why.
OUR EXAMPLE: A fictional research plan assumes consistent data definitions. Discovery of a source change justifies repairing or narrowing the study, rather than silently mixing incompatible records.

C. Changing it when a stated assumption is contradicted.

LESSON 4-1: Win rate is only half the calculation
Payoffs matter alongside probabilities.
SOURCE: Chapter 8 · Gambling and speculation | Printed pp. 228–230 | PDF pp. 254–256
KEY IDEAS:
- Labels do not determine the quality of a decision.
- Winning often can coexist with losing money.
- Evaluate probabilities together with gain and loss sizes.
BOOK CONTEXT: The chapter questions the labels “investment,” “speculation” and “gambling,” and discusses motives, emotions and avoiding ruin. The expected-value calculation below is our mathematical primer, not a formula quoted from this passage.
Combine frequency and magnitude
Expected value is the probability-weighted average payoff. In a simplified two-outcome example, multiply the win probability by the gain, then subtract the loss probability times the loss. An estimate is only as good as the assumptions and data behind it.
OUR EXAMPLE: A hypothetical result wins $1 in 80% of cases and loses $5 in 20%. Expected payoff is 0.8 × $1 − 0.2 × $5 = −$0.20 before costs.
OUR EXERCISE: Invent two hypothetical payoff distributions with the same win rate but different expected values.
SELF-CHECK: Does an 80% win rate guarantee a positive expected payoff?
A. Yes.
B. No; the loss and gain sizes also matter.
C. Only if the sample is visually convincing.
ANSWER: B. Frequent small wins can be outweighed by fewer large losses.

LESSON 4-2: The toll comes out of your edge
The contest and the cost of entering it are different things.
SOURCE: Chapter 9 · Racing and market cycles | Printed pp. 257–259 | PDF pp. 283–285
KEY IDEAS:
- Intermediary costs reduce what participants retain.
- Distinguish the headline payoff from the net payoff.
- A small gross advantage can disappear after costs.
BOOK CONTEXT: The racing discussion compares racecourse deductions with the costs borne by market participants. It draws attention to the institutions that receive payment regardless of an individual participant’s success.
Use a net result
Our illustration treats costs as a separate line. Commission, spread and other execution costs can change the conclusion even when the directional observation is correct. The historical deductions discussed in the book are not a current fee schedule.
OUR EXAMPLE: A hypothetical method produces a $12 average gross gain per completed trade. If its combined execution costs are $15, the average net result is −$3.
OUR EXERCISE: Take a paper result and add a separate cost estimate. Note which costs are known and which are assumptions.
SELF-CHECK: A $12 gross advantage with $15 in costs produces what net result?
A. $27.
B. $12.
C. −$3.
ANSWER: C. The net result subtracts costs from the gross result.

LESSON 4-3: You do not have to take every turn
Activity and opportunity are not synonyms.
SOURCE: Chapter 8 · The meaning of gambling and comparison of rules | Printed pp. 249–251 | PDF pp. 275–277
KEY IDEAS:
- Participation can be optional.
- Trade frequency does not establish an edge.
- Emotional state belongs in a decision review.
BOOK CONTEXT: The chapter’s comparison of poker and speculation includes stepping away, waiting for an opportunity and avoiding activity merely for its own sake. It also flags financial reserves and emotional strain.
Give waiting a legitimate place
A decision process that requires constant action may turn boredom into a reason to participate. Our learning exercise is to define what evidence would justify action and what would justify continuing to observe. Waiting is not proof of wisdom either; it should fit the question being studied.
OUR EXAMPLE: A student has defined an event for research. On days when the event does not occur, adding unrelated paper trades changes the experiment and obscures the result.
OUR EXERCISE: Write one condition for considering a hypothetical opportunity and one condition for waiting.
SELF-CHECK: What is a weak reason to participate?
A. The defined conditions are present.
B. The costs and uncertainty have been examined.
C. It has been a quiet day and you want action.
ANSWER: C. Boredom supplies a motive, not evidence of an opportunity.

LESSON 4-4: Racing: the favorite and the value differ
A high probability can be offered at an unattractive price.
SOURCE: Chapter 9 · Public opinion | Printed pp. 268–270 | PDF pp. 294–296

BOOK CONTEXT: The board-game preparation section recommends a personal notebook of games, including losses and draws, comparison with strong players and careful preparation. It also favors understanding over memorizing more material than one can digest.
Build a compact entry
Our journal format is deliberately small: situation, planned action, reasons, outcome and revision. The purpose is to preserve enough information to detect a recurring error, not to write a flattering narrative after every success.
OUR EXAMPLE: A fictional entry records: “I expected X because Y; I observed Z; the definition of Y was inconsistent; next time I will fix that definition first.”
OUR EXERCISE: Write one game-book entry for a recent nonfinancial decision using the five fields.
SELF-CHECK: What belongs in the record?
A. Only impressive victories.
B. Wins, losses and unresolved cases under the same format.
C. Only quotes from famous people.
ANSWER: B. A consistent record makes comparison and learning possible.

LESSON 8-5: The missing funds matter
Performance reports can favor the survivors and willing reporters.
SOURCE: Chapter 15 · Managed futures and reporting limitations | Printed pp. 476–479 | PDF pp. 502–505
KEY IDEAS:
- Ask which participants enter the reported sample.
- Include closures and withdrawals when evaluating a group.
- Distinguish reported performance from independently checked results.
BOOK CONTEXT: The author questions managed-futures performance reports despite his own participation in the industry. He discusses successful funds being more eager to report, departures from the sample and the limitations of self-reported results.
Choose the group before the outcome
An important teaching extension is to fix the cohort at the beginning and follow every member, including those that close or stop reporting. Dropping disappearing members can transform the question from “how did the group perform?” into “how did the survivors perform?”
OUR EXAMPLE: Five fictional funds start a study. Two close after large losses. Reporting only the remaining three does not describe the original five-fund experience.
OUR EXERCISE: For a performance claim, write who was eligible at the start, who disappeared and how missing observations were handled.
SELF-CHECK: Which sample better answers how the original group performed?
A. Only funds still reporting at the end.
B. The starting cohort, with closures and missing cases addressed.
C. Only the best-known winners.
ANSWER: B. Following the initial group reduces the advantage created by selective survival or reporting.

LESSON 8-6: Build a method you can explain
Join observation, incentives, probability and review into one question.
SOURCE: Chapter 16 · Epilogue | Printed pp. 507–508 | PDF pp. 533–534
KEY IDEAS:
- Use several disciplines to clarify one problem.
- Make each inference explicit.
- Finish with a question you can investigate.
BOOK CONTEXT: The epilogue brings together ecology, rhythm, competition, self-review and changing conditions through fishing. Its broad synthesis is the basis for a final exercise that joins the library’s themes.
A six-line research brief
Write the event definition, the mechanism you suspect, the participant incentives, the measurement plan, the main challenge and the revision rule. Each line should help answer the same question. Breadth is useful when it adds clarity, not when it merely supplies more metaphors.
OUR EXAMPLE: A fictional brief asks whether a defined interruption affects a later outcome. It records what counts as an interruption, why it might matter, a comparison group, costs of measurement and evidence that would contradict the claim.
OUR EXERCISE: Create your six-line brief. Then remove any line that does not help someone else examine the question.
SELF-CHECK: What is a strong final product of reading?
A. A longer list of unexamined slogans.
B. A clear question, evidence plan and revision rule.
C. Certainty that every analogy predicts markets.
ANSWER: B. A research brief turns themes into a concrete inquiry someone else can understand and challenge.
